\section{Critical Paper Review}
\label{sec:ex1}
% [6 Marks | up to 3 Hours]
% ==============================================================

\subsection*{Q1. Primary Contributions}

The paper introduces a dataset and annotation framework for detecting patronising and condescending language (PCL) in news media. PCL is a form of subtle, often well-intentioned bias that NLP had largely ignored in favour of more overt phenomena like hate speech.

The main contribution is a new annotated dataset of over 10,000 paragraphs drawn from the News on Web (NoW) corpus, retrieved via ten keywords related to vulnerable groups (e.g.\ \textit{homeless}, \textit{refugee}, \textit{immigrant}) across 20 English-speaking countries.

Alongside the dataset, they propose a two-level taxonomy of PCL categories: three high-level groups (\textit{The Saviour}, \textit{The Expert}, \textit{The Poet}) subdivided into seven fine-grained subcategories such as \textit{Unbalanced power relations}, \textit{Shallow solution}, and \textit{Metaphor}.

The paper also provides exploratory baselines using SVMs, a BiLSTM, and fine-tuned language models (BERT variants and RoBERTa) across two tasks: binary PCL detection and multi-label PCL categorisation.

\subsection*{Q2. Technical Strengths}

\paragraph{Originality and significance.} Prior work on condescension, such as Wang and Potts (2019), focused on direct interpersonal communication rather than media coverage of vulnerable groups, so the setting here is genuinely new. The two-level taxonomy also goes well beyond binary detection, offering a framework for analysing \textit{how} PCL manifests.

\paragraph{Annotation methodology.} The annotation design is careful: two primary annotators labelled the full dataset on a three-level scale, with a third resolving complete disagreements. Aggregating labels into a five-point scale is a sensible way to preserve the gradient of annotator agreement rather than discarding it. A second pass using BRAT to mark specific spans and subcategories adds fine-grained supervision that most comparable datasets lack.

\paragraph{Evaluation design.} Splitting the problem into binary detection (Task~1) and multi-label categorisation (Task~2) gives a more complete picture of what models can and cannot do. Using F1 for the positive class rather than accuracy is the right call given only 995 of 10,637 paragraphs are positive. The error analysis is one of the paper's stronger sections: concrete examples are given for both false positives (superficially PCL-like phrasing) and false negatives (categories that require world knowledge), which is more useful than just reporting aggregate numbers.

\paragraph{Accessibility.} The sociolinguistics background and taxonomy definitions are accessible to readers without prior NLP experience.

\subsection*{Q3. Key Weaknesses}

\paragraph{Limited modelling contribution.} The paper applies standard baselines (SVMs, BiLSTM, BERT variants) rather than anything designed for the PCL task. The authors note that some subcategories require world knowledge yet do not attempt to address this, for instance by incorporating external knowledge sources. The SVM-WV baseline uses averaged Word2Vec embeddings, discarding word order entirely; this choice goes unexplained despite being particularly ill-suited to detecting subtle framing.

\paragraph{Absence of ablation studies.} There are no ablations of any kind. It is unclear whether the retrieval keyword affects performance, whether the choice of annotation threshold (labels $\geq 2$ as positive) is optimal, or whether feeding the keyword as an explicit input feature would help. Without these it is hard to know which design choices actually matter.

\paragraph{Reproducibility gaps.} Several details needed for reproduction are missing: BERT fine-tuning learning rates are not reported, cross-validation fold splits are not described, and per-model preprocessing steps are not explained. The dataset is released, so approximate reproduction is possible, but getting the exact published numbers would involve significant guesswork.

\paragraph{Analysis.}

The most striking feature of the dataset is its class imbalance: only around 9.5\% of paragraphs are PCL-positive, a 9.5:1 negative-to-positive ratio. The fine-grained label distribution (panel b) shows that most positives received label 2, meaning the two primary annotators only borderline agreed. Very few paragraphs reached label 4, so a large portion of the positive class is "soft": cases where even human annotators were not confident.

Panel (c) shows that positive examples tend to be longer (median 47 vs.\ 42 words). Patronising language often involves elaboration, emotive storytelling, or framing devices that require more words, so this is unsurprising.

\paragraph{Impact Statement.}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Class-weighted training.} A naive classifier predicting "Not PCL" for every input would achieve 90.5\% accuracy, so accuracy is a misleading metric. We will use \textbf{F1 for the positive class} as the primary metric (matching the shared task) and address the imbalance during training via class-weighted cross-entropy loss or focal loss.
    \item \textbf{Tokeniser \texttt{max\_length}.} The word-count distribution informs our choice of maximum sequence length for the transformer tokeniser. Since PCL paragraphs skew longer and we do not want to truncate informative content, we should set \texttt{max\_length} generously (e.g. 256 subword tokens) and verify that truncation affects only a negligible fraction of positive examples.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Discriminative $N$-gram Analysis}

\paragraph{Visual/Tabular Evidence.}

See Figure~\ref{fig:eda2}.

    \label{fig:eda2}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Analysis.}

We compute the \textbf{log-odds ratio} of bigram frequencies between the two classes (with Laplace smoothing), measuring how much more or less likely each bigram is in patronising vs.\ non-patronising text.

The PCL-associated bigrams fall into clear \textbf{patronising archetypes} from the paper's taxonomy:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Saviour framing:} \textit{"feed hungry"}, \textit{"families help"}, \textit{"need giving"}, \textit{"giving need"}, \textit{"reaching need"}, \textit{"restore hope"}: language that positions the speaker as a rescuer.
    \item \textbf{Compassion / pity:} \textit{"feel sorry"}, \textit{"smiles faces"}, \textit{"hope hopeless"}: emotive, flowery descriptions that sentimentalise hardship.
    \item \textbf{Power asymmetry:} \textit{"burden society"}, \textit{"poverty stricken"}, \textit{"think poor"}, \textit{"little girl"}: language that reduces people to their vulnerability.
    \item \textbf{Shallow solutions / clichés:} \textit{"mother teresa"}, \textit{"developed country"}: invoking well-known figures or broad generalisations as substitutes for substantive discussion.
\end{itemize}

The Not-PCL bigrams are strikingly different in character: they are \textbf{factual, institutional, or geographic}: \textit{"said statement"}, \textit{"humanitarian assistance"}, \textit{"refugee camp"}, \textit{"social security"}, \textit{"new zealand"}, \textit{"los angeles"}, \textit{"50 000"}. Several relate to neutral reporting (\textit{"anti immigrant"}, \textit{"illegal immigrants"}, \textit{"afghan refugees"}) or clinical descriptions (\textit{"disabled list"}, \textit{"intellectually disabled"}). These bigrams describe vulnerable groups without the emotive or condescending framing that characterises PCL.

This is consistent with the paper's observation that certain PCL subcategories (\textbf{Compassion}, \textbf{Unbalanced power relations}) have recognisable lexical signatures. However, the boundary is clearly \textbf{contextual}: words like \textit{"need"}, \textit{"hope"}, and \textit{"poor"} appear on both sides, and whether they are patronising depends on the surrounding framing rather than the words themselves.

\paragraph{Impact Statement.}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Contextualised model required.} Since the same words appear in both PCL and non-PCL examples, a bag-of-words or averaged-embedding model would simply conflate them. A \textbf{contextualised language model} (e.g.\ RoBERTa) is needed to capture how framing, not just vocabulary, determines whether a passage is patronising.
    \item \textbf{Minimal text preprocessing.} The discriminative bigrams consist of common English words in specific combinations. Aggressive preprocessing (e.g. stop-word removal or lemmatisation) would destroy phrases like \textit{"feel sorry"} or \textit{"need giving"} that carry the patronising signal. We should feed \textbf{raw text} into the transformer tokeniser with no cleaning beyond what the tokeniser handles natively.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Proposed Approach}
\label{sec:ex3}
% [4 Marks | up to 2 Hours]
% ==============================================================

\subsection{Model Selection}

We fine-tune \textbf{RoBERTa-base}~\cite{liu2019roberta} (\texttt{roberta-base}, 125M parameters) for binary sequence classification.

RoBERTa is a variant of BERT trained on a much larger corpus (160\,GB) with dynamic masking and without the next-sentence prediction objective~\cite{liu2019roberta}. The key motivation here is that PCL is expressed through framing rather than specific vocabulary, so a model with weaker contextual representations would likely conflate the surface words shared between patronising and neutral examples. With only ${\sim}800$ positive training examples there is also limited room to learn representations from scratch, making strong pre-training particularly important.

\subsection{Training Methodology}

Fine-tuning involved several choices driven by the dataset properties from Section~\ref{sec:ex2}.

\paragraph{Class-weighted cross-entropy loss.} The 9.5:1 negative-to-positive imbalance means a trivial classifier achieves 90.5\% accuracy by predicting everything negative. We use balanced class weights ($w_c = N_{\text{total}} / (2 N_c)$) so that missing a positive example is penalised more than a false alarm.

\paragraph{Differential learning rates.} The classifier head uses a higher learning rate ($1 \times 10^{-4}$) than the backbone ($2 \times 10^{-5}$). Large updates to the backbone tend to overwrite the pre-trained representations, which noticeably hurt performance in early experiments.

\paragraph{Threshold tuning.}
Rather than classifying at the default threshold of 0.5, we sweep thresholds over $[0.30, 0.60]$ on the development set and select the value maximising F1 for the positive class. Given the severe class imbalance, the optimal threshold is typically below 0.5.

\paragraph{Regularisation and early stopping.}
We apply weight decay of 0.05, classifier dropout of 0.15, and gradient clipping (max norm 1.0). Early stopping with patience 5 selects the checkpoint with the best development F1 across up to 15 epochs.

\subsection*{Rationale and Expected Outcome}

Two observations from the EDA shaped this setup. First, words like \textit{``need''}, \textit{``hope''}, and \textit{``poor''} appear in both PCL and non-PCL examples, so the distinction is carried by context rather than vocabulary (Section~\ref{sec:ex2}); a bag-of-words model would be unable to capture this. Second, positive examples tend to be longer, so we set \texttt{max\_length\,=\,256} to avoid truncating content that may be discriminative.

The bigram analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:eda2}) illustrates why context matters: phrases like \textit{``feed hungry''} or \textit{``feel sorry''} are only patronising given the right surrounding structure. The original paper's own error analysis (Section~\ref{sec:ex1}) found that BERT-based models struggled in exactly these cases, and RoBERTa's larger pre-training corpus should give it somewhat better coverage of the relevant pragmatic patterns.

We expected this combination to clearly exceed the untuned RoBERTa-base baseline (F1\,=\,0.48 on dev).

\subsection{Error Analysis}

Looking at the 160 misclassified dev examples (68 FNs, 92 FPs) reveals two clear patterns, illustrated in Table~\ref{tab:errors}.

\paragraph{False negatives: PCL expressed through framing.} Most false negatives involve PCL that is implicit; the condescension comes from what the text assumes or frames rather than from any obviously emotive words.

The immigrant sheepherding example (\#1) defines immigrants purely by their lack of alternatives, but none of the individual words are emotive or condescending. The disabled artists example (\#2) positions them as needing to prove they can be talented, which annotators read as patronising; the model apparently does not, since the surface tone is enthusiastic and positive. The charity example (\#3) casts homeless people as passive recipients of someone else's ``altruistic act'', but the journalistic tone, citing a precise figure of 204,000 days of accommodation, contains nothing the model flags.

This fits the bigram EDA finding (Section~\ref{sec:ex2}) that shared vocabulary like \textit{``need''} and \textit{``hope''} appears in both classes: the model has picked up on word-level co-occurrences without learning the framing that makes them patronising.

\paragraph{False positives: keyword presence without patronising intent.} The FP pattern is more straightforward: the model seems to treat the presence of a retrieval keyword as near-sufficient evidence of PCL, regardless of context.

Example \#4 uses \textit{poor families} in a policy appeal; \#5 is arguably anti-PCL, with the writer insisting a homeless person ``was not just a faceless homeless person''; \#6 places \textit{refugees} in a statement condemning smugglers for violating human dignity. None of these are remotely patronising, but all triggered a positive prediction.

\subsection{Layer Ablation Study}

To test how much of RoBERTa's depth is actually needed, we progressively removed layers from the trained model. For each configuration we truncated the encoder to its first $N$ layers, re-tuned the decision threshold on the dev set, and evaluated without retraining. Results are in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

\begin{table}
    \centering
    \caption{Layer ablation results on the dev set. Each row uses only the first $N$ of 12 transformer layers from the trained checkpoint, evaluated with threshold tuning. $\Delta$\,F1 is relative to the full model.}
    \label{tab:ablation}
    \begin{tabular}{rcccc}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Layers} & \textbf{F1} & \textbf{Precision} & \textbf{Recall} & \textbf{$\Delta$\,F1} \\
        \midrule
        12 (full) & 0.6209 & 0.5874 & 0.6583 & --- \\
        10        & 0.6150 & 0.6852 & 0.5578 & $-$0.0059 \\
        8         & 0.4030 & 0.7826 & 0.2714 & $-$0.2179 \\
        6         & 0.2891 & 0.2055 & 0.4874 & $-$0.3317 \\
        4         & 0.1919 & 0.1065 & 0.9698 & $-$0.4289 \\
        2         & 0.2677 & 0.1625 & 0.7588 & $-$0.3531 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Analysis.} The most interesting result is how little removing the top two layers matters: F1 drops by only 0.0059 (0.6209$\to$0.6150), and precision actually goes up from 0.5874 to 0.6852. Layers 11--12 are doing very little on this task.

Below 10 layers things deteriorate quickly. At 8 layers F1 drops to 0.4030, with recall falling to 0 2714---the model becomes overly cautious and misses most positives. At 4 layers it goes the other way, essentially predicting everything as positive (recall\,=\,0.97, precision\,=\,0.11), and the 6- and 2-layer results are similarly poor.

This matches the picture from the error analysis: without enough depth the model cannot represent the contextual framing that distinguishes PCL from neutral text, and falls back on shallow keyword associations that cause either over- or under-prediction depending on the threshold.
